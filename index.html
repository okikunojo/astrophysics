Self-Study Curriculum: MIT Physics (SB, Course 8) — Focused Option

Built October 12, 2026 · 12 hours/week · starting level: high-school math and physics · free/open resources first

Read this first

  • Source of truth: the MIT Bulletin degree chart for the Focused Option (catalog.mit.edu/degree-charts/physics-course-8) and the Physics subject descriptions (catalog.mit.edu/subjects/8), both read on Oct 12, 2026. Anything I added that is not in the catalog is labeled [My addition].
  • Scope of this plan: the Departmental Program (11 required subjects + restricted electives) plus the calculus/physics prerequisites the catalog's own prerequisite fields point to. I did not plan the rest of the General Institute Requirements (chemistry, biology, 8 HASS subjects, PE/swimming) or the 48 units of unrestricted electives.
  • Headline estimate: about 3,350 study hours ≈ 278 study weeks at 12 hrs/week → roughly 5.5–6 years (realistic range 5–7 years). Section 11 shows the math.
  • Honest framing: this plan can teach you the content of the degree. It cannot give you an MIT degree, the supervised lab and research experience, or a credential. Section 1.5 lists what can't be replicated.
  • Assumptions (tell me if any are wrong): (1) you have high-school algebra and geometry, maybe some trig, but no calculus; (2) no programming background required (Python is optional enrichment); (3) you want depth over speed; (4) you can protect 12 hrs/week most weeks.

1.2 Required subjects: what they teach, why they matter

"Units" are MIT's weekly-hours measure (a 12-unit subject ≈ 12 hrs/week for a ~14-week term).

Subject Units Catalog prerequisites What it teaches (plain language) Why it matters / what you should gain Level
18.03 Differential Equations 1 12 OCW: 18.01; 18.02 as co-requisite How to write and solve equations that describe how things change over time (springs, circuits, decay, driven oscillations). Nearly every later physics subject is a differential equation in disguise. You should be able to model a system, solve it, and interpret the solution. Essential
8.03 Physics III 12 Calculus II + Physics II Vibrations, coupled oscillators and normal modes, waves, interference, diffraction, light as a wave. First place where "modes," superposition and Fourier-style thinking appear — the backbone of wave mechanics in quantum theory. Essential
8.033 Relativity 12 Calculus II + Physics II Einstein's postulates, Lorentz transformations, four-vectors, relativistic energy/momentum, intro to general relativity. The modern framework for space, time and energy; needed for particle physics and for reading modern physics at all. Essential
8.04 Quantum Physics I 12 8.03 and (18.03 or 18.032) Experimental basis of quantum physics; Schrödinger's equation in 1D and 3D; hydrogen atom; intro to spin-½. Your first real quantum mechanics. You should be able to solve standard 1D problems and explain what a wavefunction means. Essential
8.044 Statistical Physics I 12 8.03 and 18.03 Probability, thermodynamics (three laws), microcanonical and canonical ensembles; magnetism, gases, radiation, noise. Connects the microscopic to the macroscopic; the language of entropy and temperature used across physics. Essential
8.05 Quantum Physics II 12 8.04 The formal structure: vector spaces, operators, Dirac notation, uncertainty, angular momentum, entanglement, density matrices. Turns quantum mechanics from "solving equations" into a linear-algebra framework. Essential
8.06 Quantum Physics III (CI-M) 12 8.05 Perturbation theory, atomic structure, Landau levels, scattering, time-dependent perturbation theory; you write a research paper. Where quantum theory becomes a working tool for real systems. Essential
8.13 Experimental Physics I (CI-M) 18 8.04 Advanced modern-physics lab: technique, troubleshooting, data analysis, uncertainty, scientific writing/speaking. Satisfies the Institute Lab requirement. Experimental judgment — the part of physics you can't get from lectures. Essential (partly replicable — §1.5)
8.14 Experimental Physics II 18 8.05 and 8.13 Continuation with more advanced experiments plus an open-ended project taken from proposal to poster. Independent experimental design. Essential (partly replicable)
8.223 Classical Mechanics II 6 Calculus II + Physics I Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, canonical transformations, Poisson brackets. Foundations for quantum mechanics and statistical physics. Offered in IAP at MIT. Essential
8.THU Undergraduate Physics Thesis 12 none listed Research leading to an SB thesis, arranged under approved supervision. Doing and writing up original work. Essential (not replicable as supervised research)

3. Diagnostic assessment

Purpose: find gaps before you spend months in the wrong place.

Rules: Closed book, no internet, no AI. One sitting, 90 minutes, scratch paper and a basic scientific calculator allowed. Write answers before checking the key. 1 point per question (30 total). Show all work; mark any answer you guessed.

Part A — Algebra (6)

  1. Solve: 3(2x − 5) = 4x + 7.
  2. Solve: x² − 5x − 14 = 0.
  3. Solve the system: 2x + y = 7 and x − y = −1.
  4. Simplify: (x²y³)² / (x·y²).
  5. Solve the inequality: 3 − 2x ≥ 9.
  6. Solve v² = u² + 2as for s.

Part B — Functions, exponentials, logarithms (5)

  1. If f(x) = x² − 3x, find f(−2) and simplify f(x + 1).
  2. Find the domain of g(x) = √(x − 2) / (x − 5).
  3. Evaluate log₂ 40 − log₂ 5.
  4. Solve 5^(2x) = 125.
  5. A substance decays: N = 800·(½)^(t/6), t in hours. Find N at t = 18 h.

Part C — Trigonometry and geometry (5)

  1. Give sin 30°, cos 60°, tan 45°.
  2. Convert 135° to radians.
  3. A right triangle has hypotenuse 13 and one leg 5. Find the other leg and sin of the angle opposite the 5.
  4. Solve sin x = ½ for 0 ≤ x < 2π.
  5. If sin θ = 3/5 and π/2 < θ < π, find cos θ.

Part D — Vectors and units (4)

  1. For a = (3, 4): find |a| and the unit vector in its direction.
  2. Compute (2, −1, 3) · (1, 4, 2).
  3. Convert 72 km/h to m/s.
  4. With x = length, v = speed, a = acceleration, t = time, which are dimensionally consistent: (i) x = vt (ii) x = ½at (iii) x = ½at²?

Part E — High-school physics (6)

  1. A car goes from 10 m/s to 30 m/s uniformly in 5 s. Find its acceleration and distance covered.
  2. A 2 kg block feels a net force of 6 N. Find its acceleration.
  3. A 0.5 kg ball is dropped from 20 m (g = 9.8 m/s², ignore air). Find its speed at the ground.
  4. A 12 V source drives a 4 Ω and a 6 Ω resistor in series. Find the current and the voltage across the 6 Ω resistor.
  5. A wave has frequency 500 Hz and wavelength 0.68 m. Find its speed.
  6. In 2–3 sentences, explain Newton's third law with an example, naming which bodies each force acts on.

Part F — Calculus readiness (4) (informational; you may miss all of these)

  1. Find the slope of the line through (1, 2) and (4, 8).
  2. Evaluate lim (x→2) (x² − 4)/(x − 2).
  3. Find d/dx (x³) at x = 2.
  4. Evaluate ∫₀³ 2x dx.

4.2 Concurrency and cognitive load

At 12 hrs/week the default is one primary subject (~10 hrs) + ~2 hrs of spaced review of earlier material. Running two 12-unit subjects at once splits the same hours and slows both, so I only allow light pairings:

Allowed pairing (8 + 4 hrs) Why it works
18.02 (primary) with a light 8.01 review Vector skills reinforce each other.
18.03 (primary) with early 8.03 reading MIT teaches these concurrently.
18.06 (primary) with 8.033 reading Math plus lighter physics.

Avoid: 8.04 + 8.044, 8.05 + 8.06, 8.07 + 8.09 simultaneously. They are derivation-heavy and compete for the same working memory.

Every stage ends with a consolidation week (cumulative exam, remediation, retention retest of the previous stage) — see §7 and §9.

5. Course-by-course study roadmap

How to read each card: Needs = prior knowledge. Resources = one primary plus at most two supplements (free unless marked paid). Mastery = measurable gate (full framework in §9). Hours are my estimates at your 12-hrs/week pace.

Note on versions: MIT OCW hosts older offerings (e.g., 8.02 from 2007, 8.033 from 2006, 8.04/8.05 from 2013–2016). The 2026 catalog descriptions are your target syllabus; OCW is the delivery vehicle. Where OCW is missing something the catalog lists, I say so.

Standard weekly task pattern for every course (details in §7): 2 learn sessions → 2 problem sessions → 1 recall session → 1 closed-book quiz + error analysis + applied task → 1 weekly review.

Stage 0 — Foundations Bridge [My addition] · ~130 hrs · ~11 weeks

  • Purpose: make calculus and mechanics learnable.
  • Needs: arithmetic, basic algebra.
  • Objectives: manipulate algebra fluently; work with functions, exponentials, logs, trig; use vectors and units; understand "rate of change" intuitively.
  • Topic order: 0A Algebra (~20 h): linear equations, rearranging formulas, exponent laws, radicals, quadratics, systems, inequalities → 0B Functions, exponentials, logs (~30 h): notation, domain/range, transformations, inverses, polynomial and rational behavior, exponential growth/decay, log laws → 0C Trigonometry (~30 h): right triangles, unit circle, radians, graphs, identities, inverse trig, solving trig equations → 0D Vectors and units (~15 h): components, dot product, unit conversion, dimensional analysis, scientific notation, significant figures → 0E Rates and limits intuition (~15 h): slope, secant to tangent, average vs instantaneous rate, the limit idea → 0F Mechanics warm-up (~10 h) → checkpoints (~10 h).
  • Resources: Primary: Khan Academy (free) — algebra → precalculus path (khanacademy.org). Supplement: OpenStax University Physics Vol. 1, Ch. 1–3 (units, vectors, 1-D motion) (free, link).
  • Practice: ≥ 25 problems per topic; flashcards for every error.
  • Project: spreadsheet model of exponential decay (week 3 of the plan).
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on a closed-book 90-min cumulative exam; ≥ 90% on a retake of diagnostic A–D.
  • Pitfalls: algebra slips masquerading as concept errors (track them separately in your error log); skipping trig identities now and paying for it in 8.03.

Stage 1 — 18.01 Single-Variable Calculus · ~165 hrs · ~14 weeks

  • Purpose: the language of change.
  • Needs: Stage 0.
  • Objectives: differentiate and integrate fluently; set up optimization and related-rates problems; use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
  • Topic order (follow OCW's unit order): limits and derivatives → differentiation rules → applications (linearization, optimization, related rates) → integration and the Fundamental Theorem → integration techniques → applications of integration → infinite series (brief, per the course description).
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 18.01SC (free): lecture videos, recitation videos, notes, problem sets and exams with solutions (link). Supplement: Khan Academy AP Calculus AB (free).
  • Weekly tasks: watch one session set, do its worked examples, then the problem set before looking at solutions.
  • Assessments: weekly 10-question quizzes; OCW exams under timed, closed-book conditions.
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on each OCW exam; 20 mixed derivatives/integrals in 30 min at ≥ 90%.
  • Pitfalls: treating rules as recipes (always ask "what quantity is changing?"); neglecting algebra clean-up on integrals.

Stage 2 — 8.01 Classical Mechanics · ~185 hrs · ~15 weeks

  • Purpose: learn to model the physical world quantitatively.
  • Needs: derivatives and integrals (first ~60% of Stage 1), vectors.
  • Objectives: set up free-body diagrams; use energy, momentum and angular-momentum conservation; solve rotational and oscillatory problems.
  • Topic order (catalog + OCW): kinematics (1-D, 2-D) → Newton's laws and applications → work, energy, potential energy → momentum, collisions, center of mass → rotation, torque, angular momentum → oscillations → central forces and gravitation → fluids.
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.01SC (free; lectures, problem sets, open-textbook chapters) (link). Supplement: OpenStax University Physics Vol. 1 (free).
  • Practice: 4–6 multi-step problems per week; always solve symbolically first, then plug in numbers.
  • Project: projectile motion with air drag in a spreadsheet (or Python if you pick it up).
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on closed-book cumulative exams you build from OCW problem sets and OpenStax review problems (check the course page for any exams); derive the key results (energy conservation from Newton's second law, rotational analog) from a blank page.
  • Pitfalls: formula-hunting without a diagram; sign errors in vector components.

Stage 3 — 18.02 Multivariable Calculus · ~165 hrs · ~14 weeks

  • Purpose: calculus in 2-D and 3-D, needed for fields.
  • Needs: 18.01.
  • Objectives: partial derivatives and optimization; multiple integrals; line/surface integrals and the major theorems of vector calculus.
  • Topic order (OCW course description: differential, integral and vector calculus): vectors and matrices → partial derivatives and optimization → multiple integrals → vector calculus (line integrals, flux, Green's, divergence and Stokes' theorems).
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 18.02SC (free) (link). OCW states MIT expects about 150 hours on this course.
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on OCW exams; set up (not just evaluate) 10 integrals in different coordinate systems.
  • Pitfalls: visualizing regions of integration — sketch every one.

Stage 4 — 8.02 Electricity and Magnetism · ~185 hrs · ~15 weeks

  • Purpose: the first complete field theory.
  • Needs: 18.01 and 8.01 (18.02 helps a lot).
  • Objectives: compute electric and magnetic fields and potentials; apply Faraday's and Ampère's laws; reach Maxwell's equations and EM waves.
  • Topic order (catalog): charge and Coulomb's law → electrostatic field and potential → conductors and dielectrics → electrostatic energy → currents and circuits → magnetic fields and Ampère's law → magnetic materials → Faraday induction → EM waves and Maxwell's equations.
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.02, Spring 2007 (free; lecture notes, problem sets, online textbook) (link; course path confirmed via its resource pages). Supplements: Walter Lewin's 8.02 video lectures, Spring 2002 (free, link); OpenStax University Physics Vol. 2 (free; same site as Vol. 1, link not individually verified).
  • Project: Faraday demo — drop a magnet through a coil and record the voltage spike with a multimeter or audio input.
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on a cumulative closed-book exam; derive the field of a charged line and plate using Gauss's law unaided.
  • Pitfalls: mixing up line, surface and volume integrals; skipping the physical picture before the math.

Stage 5 — 18.03 Differential Equations · ~165 hrs · ~14 weeks

  • Purpose: the tool for oscillators, circuits and quantum dynamics.
  • Needs: 18.01; 18.02 (OCW calls it a co-requisite).
  • Objectives: solve first- and second-order linear ODEs and 2×2 systems; use complex exponentials; interpret solutions physically.
  • Topic order (OCW): first-order equations and modeling → complex numbers and exponentials → second-order constant-coefficient equations (homogeneous, driven, resonance) → 2×2 systems and phase portraits. Check the course's unit list for Fourier and Laplace-transform units and include them.
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 18.03SC (free; Mattuck lectures, notes, recitation videos, exams with solutions) (link).
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on OCW exams; solve a driven damped oscillator and explain resonance in words.
  • Pitfalls: memorizing solution forms without understanding where the exponentials come from.

Stage 6 — 8.03 Vibrations and Waves · ~185 hrs · ~15 weeks

  • Purpose: waves, modes and interference.
  • Needs: 18.02, 8.02; 18.03 helps.
  • Objectives: analyze coupled oscillators and normal modes; derive the wave equation; analyze interference and diffraction.
  • Topic order (catalog): simple harmonic motion → forced vibrations and resonance → coupled oscillators and normal modes → continuous systems → wave reflection/refraction, phase and group velocity → EM waves and polarization → interference, Huygens, Fraunhofer diffraction, gratings.
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.03SC (free; lecture videos, notes, problem sets, exams) (link). Supplement: OpenStax Vol. 1, Ch. 15–17 (oscillations, waves, sound).
  • Project: measure the damped oscillation of a spring/phone-accelerometer system and fit it to a damped-oscillator model from 18.03.
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on OCW exams; find normal modes of a 2- and 3-mass system from scratch.
  • Pitfalls: complex-number bookkeeping and Fourier ideas arrive quickly — slow down on those sessions.

Stage 7 — 18.06 Linear Algebra [My addition] · ~150 hrs · ~13 weeks

  • Purpose: the math language of quantum mechanics.
  • Needs: algebra and 18.02 comfort (OCW says calculus knowledge isn't required to learn it).
  • Objectives: solve linear systems, understand the four subspaces, compute eigenvalues/eigenvectors, orthogonality and projections.
  • Topic order (OCW): Ax = b and the four subspaces → orthogonality and least squares → determinants → eigenvalues and eigenvectors → similar matrices and Jordan form → check the unit list for symmetric matrices and SVD.
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 18.06SC (free; Strang lectures, problem-solving videos, exams with solutions) (link). Supplement (paid): Strang, Introduction to Linear Algebra (the course follows it).
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on OCW exams; diagonalize a 3×3 symmetric matrix by hand and explain each step geometrically.
  • Pitfalls: treating matrices as grids of numbers instead of linear maps.

Stage 8 — 8.033 Relativity · ~170 hrs · ~14 weeks

  • Purpose: the modern view of space, time and energy.
  • Needs: 18.02, 8.02.
  • Objectives: use Lorentz transformations and four-vectors; analyze relativistic collisions; handle paradoxes; understand the basics of general relativity.
  • Topic order (OCW syllabus): symmetry and invariance, Einstein's postulates → Lorentz transformation, Minkowski diagrams → time dilation, length contraction, spacetime intervals → four-vectors, momentum, energy, mass → relativistic collisions → relativity and electromagnetism → equivalence principle → cosmology and black holes (Schwarzschild metric, gravitational redshift).
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.033, Fall 2006 (free; lecture notes, problem sets, exams) (link).
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on OCW exams; solve three paradox problems (twin, pole-and-barn, Terrell-type) with spacetime diagrams.
  • Pitfalls: mixing frames mid-calculation — label every frame.

Stage 9 — 8.04 Quantum Physics I · ~185 hrs · ~15 weeks

  • Purpose: first real quantum mechanics.
  • Needs: 8.03, 18.03; 18.06 helps.
  • Objectives: explain the experimental basis; solve Schrödinger's equation in 1-D potentials; understand the hydrogen atom and the uncertainty principle.
  • Topic order (catalog): photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, de Broglie waves → wave-packets and Fourier representation → Schrödinger's equation and probability amplitudes → stationary states → 1-D problems (barriers, wells, delta functions, harmonic oscillator) → 3-D central potentials and hydrogen → intro to spin-½.
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.04, Spring 2016 (Zwiebach; lectures, notes, problem sets, exam) (link). Supplement (paid): Zwiebach, Mastering Quantum Mechanics (MIT Press, 2022) — OCW states these lectures form its basis.
  • Project: numerically solve the 1-D time-independent Schrödinger equation for a finite well (spreadsheet or Python).
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on a closed-book exam built from the OCW problem sets and final; derive barrier tunneling probability and the harmonic-oscillator ground state from scratch.
  • Pitfalls: "what is the wavefunction?" confusion — keep a one-page glossary of measurement postulates.

Stage 10 — 8.223 Classical Mechanics II · ~95 hrs · ~8 weeks

  • Purpose: Lagrangian/Hamiltonian formalism.
  • Needs: 8.01, 18.02.
  • Objectives: set up Lagrangians for constrained systems; use conservation laws; move to Hamiltonian mechanics.
  • Topic order (OCW outline): Lagrangian mechanics (lectures 1–4) → conserved quantities → orbits and scattering → oscillations → Hamiltonian mechanics (lectures 15–19).
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.223, IAP 2017 (free; lecture notes, problem sets and solutions, projects) (link). Required text (paid): Landau & Lifshitz, Mechanics (3rd ed.); suggested (paid): Thornton & Marion; Goldstein.
  • Project: the course asks students to build a small trebuchet and demonstrate it — a nice hands-on option.
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on a problem-set-based cumulative exam; derive Euler–Lagrange equations from a variational statement.
  • Pitfalls: the course is compressed (IAP pacing) — stretch it to ~8 weeks and don't skip Hamiltonian lectures.

Stage 11 — 8.044 Statistical Physics I · ~185 hrs · ~15 weeks

  • Purpose: entropy, temperature and ensembles.
  • Needs: 8.03, 18.03 (OCW lists both); 18.02 partial derivatives.
  • Objectives: apply probability and ensembles to physical systems; use the three laws of thermodynamics; connect microscopic and macroscopic descriptions.
  • Topic order (catalog): probability and random variables → thermodynamic equilibrium and macroscopic variables → microcanonical ensemble → canonical ensemble → laws of thermodynamics → applications (magnetism, polyatomic gases, thermal radiation, electrons in solids, noise).
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.044, Spring 2013 (Greytak; notes, problem sets, exams with solutions) (link). The syllabus lists recommended texts (see its reading list). Supplement (paid, [my suggestion]): Schroeder, An Introduction to Thermal Physics.
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on OCW exams; derive the Boltzmann factor and the ideal-gas equation of state from the canonical ensemble.
  • Pitfalls: partial-derivative bookkeeping in thermodynamics — practice Maxwell relations explicitly.

Stage 12 — 8.05 Quantum Physics II · ~190 hrs · ~16 weeks

  • Purpose: the formal structure of quantum mechanics.
  • Needs: 8.04, 18.06.
  • Objectives: work fluently in Dirac notation; analyze spin systems and angular momentum; understand entanglement and decoherence at an introductory level.
  • Topic order (catalog): vector spaces and operators → inner products, adjoints → commutators → Dirac bra-kets → uncertainty principle → spectral theorem and commuting observables → Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures → axioms → coherent states and NMR → multiparticle states → teleportation, EPR, Bell → angular momentum and addition → density matrices.
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.05, Fall 2013 (Zwiebach; lectures, notes, assignments, exams) (link). Supplement (paid): Zwiebach, Mastering Quantum Mechanics.
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on closed-book exams; prove the generalized uncertainty principle and compute spin-½ rotations on a blank page.
  • Pitfalls: abstraction overload — always map operators back to matrices for a concrete test case.

Stage 13 — 8.06 Quantum Physics III · ~190 hrs · ~16 weeks

  • Purpose: quantum mechanics as a working tool.
  • Needs: 8.05 (OCW requires a C or better in it).
  • Objectives: use perturbation theory and variational methods; understand fine structure, Zeeman/Stark effects, scattering; write a short research-style paper.
  • Topic order (catalog): natural units and scales → time-independent perturbation theory (degenerate and non-degenerate), variational method → one- and two-electron atoms, fine structure → Zeeman and Stark effects → Landau levels and the integer quantum Hall effect → scattering and partial waves → time-dependent perturbation theory → term paper.
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.06, Spring 2018 (lecture notes, videos, problem sets, term-paper materials; exams are not available to OCW users, per the syllabus) (link). The syllabus lists reference texts including Griffiths' Introduction to Quantum Mechanics (paid).
  • Assessment substitute: build your own closed-book exams from problem sets 1–9 (rotate which sets you exclude).
  • Project: the course's term-paper assignment — write a short journal-style paper on a quantum system outside the regular coursework.
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on your self-built exams; term paper reviewed by an outside reader.
  • Pitfalls: perturbation-theory algebra is long — organize with a notation sheet.

Stage 14 — 8.07 Electromagnetism II (elective) · ~185 hrs · ~15 weeks

  • Purpose: advanced electrodynamics.
  • Needs: 8.03, 18.03 (catalog).
  • Topics (catalog): electrostatics and magnetostatics → EM properties of matter → time-dependent fields and Maxwell's equations → EM waves, emission, absorption, scattering → relativistic electrodynamics and mechanics.
  • Resources: Catalog description as syllabus. I did not verify an OCW offering for 8.07 — search OCW before relying on one. Supplement (paid, [my suggestion]): Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics.
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on problem sets done without solutions; derive the retarded potentials and Larmor radiation from scratch.
  • Pitfalls: vector-calculus identities — keep a cheat sheet and drill it.

Stage 15 — 8.09 Classical Mechanics III (elective) · ~185 hrs · ~15 weeks

  • Purpose: advanced mechanics and nonlinear dynamics.
  • Needs: 8.223 (catalog).
  • Topics (catalog): Lagrangian/Hamiltonian mechanics with constraints → rigid body dynamics → vibrations → central forces → Hamilton–Jacobi and action-angle variables → perturbation theory → continuous systems → ideal and viscous fluids, turbulence → nonlinear dynamics and chaos.
  • Resources: Catalog description as syllabus. I did not verify an OCW offering for 8.09. Use the Landau–Lifshitz and Goldstein texts from the 8.223 syllabus (paid).
  • Mastery: ≥ 85% on problem sets done without solutions; simulate a chaotic pendulum and explain Lyapunov behavior qualitatively.

Stage 16 — Experimental track (partial substitute for 8.13/8.14) [My addition] · ~200 hrs (150–300) · ~17 weeks

  • Purpose: experimental technique and scientific writing.
  • Needs: 8.04 and 8.05 (matches the catalog prerequisites for 8.13 and 8.14).
  • Objectives: design a measurement; quantify uncertainty; fit and test models; write a short paper and give a short talk.
  • What I recommend (low-cost, safe): error analysis and curve-fitting practice; damped oscillator with phone sensors; interference and diffraction with a laser pointer (Class 2 only) and a diffraction grating; spectroscopy of lamps with a grating; analysis of public datasets (the catalog's 8.16 mentions LIGO open data as a module example). Read the OCW Junior Lab guides to see how professional labs are structured.
  • Resources: Primary: OCW 8.13–14 "Junior Lab" (syllabus, experiment guides, schedule summaries) (link). Note that OCW says some on-campus instructions don't apply to OCW users.
  • Safety: do not attempt experiments involving radioactive sources, Class 3b/4 lasers, high voltage or biological materials without proper supervision — the OCW syllabus itself requires formal training for them.
  • Deliverables: 4–6 lab reports in Physical Review Letters style, one 10-minute recorded talk.
  • Mastery: each report passes a rubric (clear hypothesis, uncertainty analysis, reproducible methods, honest discussion of limitations); one outside reader's feedback incorporated.
  • This is a partial substitute: it builds skills, not the hands-on access MIT's lab provides.

Stage 17 — Thesis capstone (substitute for 8.THU) · ~150 hrs (120–200) · ~13 weeks

  • Purpose: carry a question from idea to written result.
  • Needs: everything above.
  • Deliverable: an 8–15 page paper replicating or extending a published result (computational, theoretical, or analysis of public data), with figures, uncertainty analysis, and references.
  • Process: proposal (week 1) → literature scan (weeks 1–3) → work (weeks 4–10) → draft (week 11) → outside review (week 12) → revision (week 13).
  • Mastery: an outside reader with physics background confirms the logic and methods; you can defend every equation in a 20-minute recorded talk.
  • Honest limit: this is not supervised research and does not replace 8.THU's institutional role.

7.3 Routine for every session type

Learn session (2 h). (1) 5-min preview: scan headings and write 3 questions. (2) Watch or read in 10–15 min chunks, pausing to predict the next step. (3) After each chunk, close the source and write the key idea in your own words. (4) Last 15 min: write a one-paragraph summary and add terms to your glossary.

Problem session (1.5–2 h). (1) Attempt each problem for 10–15 min before looking at any hint or solution. (2) If stuck, write what you tried, then check a hint (not the solution). (3) After solving, rewrite the solution in clean steps. (4) Log every error with a tag: concept / setup / algebra / calculus / careless.

Recall session (1 h). Blank-page retrieval of key definitions and derivations, then flashcards (spaced intervals: day 1, 3, 7, 14, 30, 60, 120 after first review). Anything you can't recall gets a new card.

Quiz block (Saturday). Closed book, timed, 8–10 questions covering the week plus 2–3 questions from earlier weeks (interleaving). Score immediately, then do error analysis.

Weekly review (Sunday, 1 h). Score the quiz, update the tracker (actual hours, status, gaps), write three lines: what went well, what slowed me down, what changes next week.

7.4 Progress tracking each week

Record: planned vs actual hours, problems completed, quiz score, number of new errors by tag, mastery status (§10), and one energy rating (1–5).

7.5 Handling a difficult topic without falling behind

  1. Two-pass rule: after two full attempts (learn + problems), move on if the weekly plan allows; flag the topic "Needs Review."
  2. Trace the error. Is it concept, math tool, or prerequisite? Fix the tool first (e.g., a trig identity), then the concept.
  3. Switch format: a different explanation (OpenStax text instead of the lecture; a worked example instead of a derivation).
  4. Cap the overrun: if a topic uses 1.5× its planned hours, stop, do a 30-minute triage (§12.1), and either shorten the scope or schedule the topic into the consolidation week.
  5. Never let two consecutive weeks end with the same unresolved gap.

7.6 Revision, assessment and catch-up weeks

  • Every stage ends with a consolidation week (see the 12-hour distribution above): cumulative exam, remediation, and a retention retest of the previous stage.
  • Monthly review (week 4, 8, 12…): swap one Saturday project hour for the four-week review (§12.3).
  • Catch-up buffer: my timeline includes 23 extra weeks beyond the ~255 content weeks; if you fall behind, use those buffer weeks before extending the calendar.
  • Planned rest: one lighter week (6 hours, review only) every ~12 weeks is built into those buffer weeks.

9. Assessment and mastery framework

9.1 Diagnostic tests before each stage

Before starting a stage, do a 30-minute closed-book pretest of 10 questions on its prerequisites (build it from the previous stage's problem sets). < 70% → remediate prerequisites before starting.

9.2 Within each learning unit

  • Weekly closed-book quiz (8–10 questions, 30–60 min). Pass = ≥ 80%.
  • Problem sets: solve before looking at solutions. Pass = ≥ 80% of core problems correct on the first honest attempt (hints count as half credit).
  • Explain-it test: record yourself explaining one derivation for 5–10 minutes with no notes; if you stall, the topic isn't mastered.

9.3 Cumulative exams (end of each stage)

Closed-book, timed (normal exam length: 2–3 hours), built from the OCW exams where they exist, or from problem sets when they don't (8.06, 8.07, 8.09). Pass = ≥ 85%.

9.4 Integrative projects (multi-course)

After stage Project

9 Numerical solution of 1-D Schrödinger equation for a finite well; compare with analytic bound-state conditions.
11 Simulation of an ideal gas or a 2-D spin lattice; compare with partition-function predictions.

9.5 Periodic review of older material

Every Friday recall session includes at least 20% questions from material older than 4 weeks. Every consolidation week includes a retention retest of the previous stage (target ≥ 75% without review).

9.6 Mastery bands and remediation

Score on a closed-book assessment Status Action
≥ 85% Mastered (if retention retest ≥ 75% four weeks later) Advance.
70–84% Needs Review Targeted remediation (below); retest in 7–10 days.
< 70% Not mastered Re-study the unit with a remediation plan; retest in 10–14 days.
Two failed retests Prerequisite gap Go back to the prerequisite stage's diagnostic and relearn the missing tool before returning.

Remediation process:

  1. Classify every missed question: concept, setup, algebra, calculus/tool, careless.
  2. Map each to a specific topic in the tracker's "Knowledge gaps" column.
  3. Fix the tool first. If errors are math skills, drill those before the concept.
  4. Relearn the topic from a different source or format.
  5. Practice on fresh problems (not the ones you failed).
  6. Retest after at least 3 days using a parallel version.

Watching lectures or finishing a textbook is never evidence of mastery. Only closed-book performance and the explain-it test count.
